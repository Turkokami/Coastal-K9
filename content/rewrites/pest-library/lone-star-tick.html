Lone Star Tick — Identification & Control

The lone star tick is the most commonly encountered tick in much of South Carolina. It is an aggressive, fast-moving tick that actively hunts people and pets along wooded edges, tall grass and brushy yards — and it's the tick most associated with alpha-gal syndrome, an allergy to red meat.

Quick answer: a reddish-brown tick with a single white dot on its back is an adult female lone star tick. Remove attached ticks promptly with fine-tipped tweezers and talk to a doctor if a rash, fever or unusual reaction follows.

How to identify the lone star tick

FeatureWhat to look for
Adult femaleReddish-brown with one white or silvery dot in the middle of the back
Adult maleReddish-brown with faint white streaks or spots around the edge
Nymphs & larvaeVery small; larvae ("seed ticks") often attack in large groups
Look-alikesAmerican dog tick (ornate white markings), blacklegged/deer tick (smaller, dark legs, no spot)

Behavior and biology

Lone star ticks feed on deer, turkeys, other wildlife, pets and people. Unlike ticks that wait on vegetation, they move across the ground toward hosts. Activity runs from early spring into fall in the Southeast, with adults and nymphs in spring and summer and swarms of larvae in late summer. Deer moving through wooded lots and pasture edges carry them right up to yards.

Why lone star ticks matter

The CDC links lone star ticks to ehrlichiosis, tularemia and STARI, and to alpha-gal syndrome — a delayed allergic reaction to red meat that can be serious. Health questions belong with your doctor; our job is to reduce tick numbers where your family and pets spend time.

Prevention you can do

  • Keep grass mowed and clear brush and leaf litter at the edge of the lawn.
  • Create a dry mulch or gravel barrier between wooded areas and the yard.
  • Keep pets on veterinarian-recommended tick prevention.
  • Check yourself, children and pets after time outdoors.

Frequently asked questions

Do lone star ticks carry Lyme disease?

Lyme disease is spread by the blacklegged (deer) tick, not the lone star tick. Lone star ticks are linked to other illnesses and to alpha-gal syndrome.

When are ticks worst in the CSRA?

Spring through early fall, with peaks in late spring and early summer.
