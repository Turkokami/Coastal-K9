Eastern Mole — Identification & Control

The eastern mole tunnels just below the surface of CSRA lawns, leaving raised ridges that collapse underfoot. Moles rarely come above ground and eat earthworms and insect larvae — not plant roots — so the damage is mostly the tunneling itself.

Quick answer: raised, winding ridges in the lawn are mole tunnels. Small surface runways and chewed plant roots point to voles instead — a different animal with a different fix.

How to identify mole activity

FeatureMoleVole (look-alike)
AnimalVelvety gray fur, no visible eyes or ears, huge paddle-like front feet, pointed naked snoutMouse-like rodent with small eyes and ears
SignsRaised surface ridges; volcano-shaped dirt mounds from deeper tunnelsNarrow surface runways in grass, small round holes, chewed roots and bark
DietEarthworms, grubs, insect larvaePlants, roots, bulbs

Behavior and biology

Moles are active year-round. They build shallow feeding tunnels near the surface — often after rain, when soil is soft — plus deeper travel tunnels they use repeatedly. A single mole can create a surprising amount of tunneling, so damage often looks like many more moles than are actually present.

What doesn't work

Castor-oil products, vibrating stakes, chewing gum and similar remedies have little evidence behind them. Treating grubs alone rarely removes moles, because earthworms are a major food source.

Frequently asked questions

Will killing grubs get rid of moles?

Not by itself. Moles eat a lot of earthworms, so grub control helps only as part of a removal plan.
