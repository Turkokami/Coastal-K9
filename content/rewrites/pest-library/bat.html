Bat — Identification & Exclusion

Bats are some of the most valuable animals in the CSRA — a single colony eats huge numbers of night-flying insects — but a colony in an attic, wall or soffit brings droppings (guano), odor, and a rabies-exposure risk for the people living below it. The answer is to get the bats out humanely and close the house behind them, never to kill them.

Quick answer: bats are removed with one-way exclusion devices that let them leave and not return — timed to avoid the summer season when flightless pups are in the roost. Never handle a bat with bare hands, and if a bat is found in a room where someone was sleeping, call your doctor or the health department.

SpeciesNotes

Brazilian (Mexican) free-tailed batTail extends past the wing membrane; forms large, smelly colonies in roofs and walls
Evening batSmall and brown; uses buildings and trees in summer

South Carolina is home to more than a dozen bat species; most roost in trees, caves and bridges rather than houses.

Signs of bats in a building

  • Bats emerging from a roofline, gable vent or chimney at dusk.
  • Droppings piling up on attic insulation, porches, window sills or below an entry gap — they crumble when crushed, unlike rodent droppings.
  • Dark rub stains around a gap in soffits, trim or flashing.
  • Squeaking or scratching in walls at dusk and dawn; a strong ammonia smell.

Why bats in the house are a problem

Bats are one of the main rabies carriers in South Carolina, and their guano can harbor the fungus that causes histoplasmosis. Accumulated droppings also stain and damage insulation and ceilings.

How we handle bats

  1. Inspect and confirm

    We find every active and potential entry point and confirm the species and colony size.

  2. Time it right

    Exclusion is scheduled outside the maternity season so no flightless young are trapped inside.

  3. One-way exclusion

    Devices on the active entries let bats leave and not return; every other gap is sealed.

  4. Seal and clean up

    Once the colony is out, entries are permanently closed and contaminated insulation can be addressed.

Frequently asked questions

Can I just seal the holes?

Not while bats are inside. Sealing traps them, and they'll find another way into the living space or die in the walls. Exclusion first, then sealing.

Is it legal to kill bats?

No — bats are protected, and killing them isn't necessary. Humane exclusion solves the problem.
