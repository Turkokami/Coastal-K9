Nine-Banded Armadillo — Identification & Control

Nine-banded armadillos have spread steadily north and east across the Southeast and are now regulars in parts of the CSRA. They dig — rooting up lawns and flower beds for grubs and insects, and burrowing under slabs, sheds, porches and foundations.

Quick answer: lots of small, cone-shaped holes in the lawn overnight, plus a larger burrow near a structure, point to an armadillo. Exclusion around the burrow site and removal stop the damage.

How to identify armadillo damage

FeatureWhat to look for
AnimalGray-brown armored shell with bands, long tail, pointed snout, about 2.5 feet long
Lawn damageShallow, cone-shaped holes a few inches across where it rooted for insects
BurrowsOpenings about 7–8 inches wide, often under slabs, sheds, stumps or brush

Behavior and biology

Armadillos feed mostly on insects and grubs, mainly at night or around dusk in warm weather. They have poor eyesight but a strong sense of smell. Females usually give birth to identical quadruplets.

Why armadillos are a problem

Rooting damages turf and beds, and burrows can undermine slabs, walkways and foundations. Armadillos are also known to carry the bacterium that causes leprosy (Hansen's disease), so they shouldn't be handled.
