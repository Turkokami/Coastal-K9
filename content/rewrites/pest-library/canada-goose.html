Canada Goose — Identification & Control

The Canada goose is the large black-necked goose with the white chinstrap that has settled year-round on CSRA ponds, golf courses, HOA lakes, school fields and office parks. Many of these birds no longer migrate at all — they are resident flocks that grow every year where the grass is short and the water is open.

Quick answer: Canada geese are federally protected, so you can't harm them, take eggs or destroy active nests without federal authorization. What you can do legally is make the property feel unsafe — and a working dog is the one deterrent geese don't learn to ignore.

How to identify the Canada goose

FeatureWhat to look for
SizeLarge waterfowl, roughly 2.5–3.5 feet long
MarkingsBlack head and neck with a white "chinstrap" patch; brown-gray body, pale chest, white under the tail
YoungGoslings are yellow-green and fluffy in spring
Look-alikesDomestic geese and goose hybrids on park ponds (often white or mottled, with orange bills)

Behavior and biology

Resident Canada geese pair for life and return to the same property each year. In the Southeast they nest in early spring, usually close to water on islands, banks and landscaped beds; incubation takes about four weeks. Pairs defend the nest hard — hissing, wing-beating and charging people who get close.

In early summer adults go through a molt and lose their flight feathers for several weeks. Flocks gather on ponds with open lawn beside them, because short, fertilized grass is ideal food and open water lets them escape predators. Every successful brood adds to next year's flock.

Why geese are a problem

  • Droppings. A single adult goose produces a large volume of droppings every day; a flock fouls walkways, docks, beaches, greens and athletic fields, and adds nutrients that feed algae in ponds.
  • Aggression. Nesting geese charge residents, golfers, employees and children — a liability for HOAs, campuses and businesses.
  • Turf and water damage. Grazing and droppings damage turf; flocks can degrade water quality in retention ponds.
  • Safety. Geese crossing roads and parking lots, and near airfields, create collision risks.

What doesn't work for long

Plastic coyotes, swan decoys, flags and noise makers work briefly — until the geese learn they never move. Feeding bans help but don't move an established flock. Chemical grazing repellents need repeated application. And anything involving eggs or nests requires federal registration and specific timing, so it is not a do-it-yourself job.

Prevention you can do

  • Stop all feeding of waterfowl on the property, and post signs.
  • Let a strip of taller native grass or shrubs grow along the shoreline — geese avoid cover where a predator could hide.
  • Reduce mowed lawn running straight down to the water's edge.
  • Start deterrence before nesting season, not after nests are built.

How we handle Canada geese

We use trained working dogs to haze geese — the dog moves the flock off the property without contact or harm, and repeated visits teach the geese the site isn't safe to nest or feed on. Dog hazing is a legal, non-lethal method that doesn't require a permit. We schedule visits more often around spring nesting and the summer molt and adjust as goose pressure drops. Frequently asked questions

Is it legal to chase geese with a dog?

Yes. Harassing Canada geese to move them is legal as long as the birds aren't harmed or captured and nests and eggs aren't disturbed. That is exactly how a professional hazing dog works.

Can I remove a goose nest myself?

No — not without federal authorization. Nest and egg management for resident Canada geese requires registration with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and follows specific rules.

When should goose control start?

Before nesting season. Geese that have already built a nest are far harder to move than birds that are still choosing a site.
