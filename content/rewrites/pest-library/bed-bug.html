Bed Bug — Identification & Control

Bed bugs are small, flat, blood-feeding insects that hide in beds, furniture and wall cracks and come out at night to feed. They are among the hardest household pests to catch early, which is exactly why a trained dog is so valuable: a certified canine team can screen a room in minutes and alert on live bugs and viable eggs a visual check misses.

Quick answer: if you are waking up with itchy welts in lines or clusters and find rusty spots on mattress seams, treat it as a possible bed bug problem — don't move to another room or spray store-bought insecticide. Both spread the infestation. Get the room screened first.

How to identify a bed bug

FeatureWhat you'll see
SizeAdults about 3/16 inch (5 mm) — the size of an apple seed. Newly hatched nymphs are about 1/16 inch and nearly clear.
Shape & colorFlat and oval when unfed; reddish-brown. After feeding they swell, lengthen and darken.
WingsNone. Bed bugs crawl; they do not fly or jump.
EggsPearl-white, about 1 mm, glued into seams and crevices — very hard to see without magnification.
Common look-alikesBat bugs and swallow bugs (nearly identical, tied to roosts and nests), carpet beetles, and booklice.

Life cycle and behavior

A bed bug passes through five nymph stages, and each one needs a blood meal before it can molt to the next. At normal indoor temperatures the trip from egg to adult takes roughly five weeks, and a mated female can lay several eggs a day for months — a few hundred over her life. Eggs hatch in about one to two weeks.

They are nocturnal and stay close to where people rest: mattress and box-spring seams, bed frames, headboards, nightstands, upholstered chairs, and baseboards. As numbers grow they spread into outlets, picture frames and wall voids, and into neighboring rooms or units. Adults can survive for months between meals, so an empty room is not a cleared room.

Why bed bugs are a problem in the CSRA

Bed bugs are not known to spread disease, but the bites, the sleep loss and the cost of getting rid of them are real. Aiken's equestrian season, North Augusta and Augusta hotel traffic, Fort Eisenhower moves, college housing in Aiken and Columbia, and the region's senior-living communities all move people and belongings in and out constantly — and bed bugs travel in luggage, used furniture and laundry. Many populations in the U.S. are also resistant to the pyrethroid sprays sold over the counter, which is why do-it-yourself treatment so often makes a problem bigger rather than smaller.

Signs of an infestation

  • Rusty or dark spots on sheets and along mattress seams — digested blood.
  • Itchy welts in a line or cluster that appear overnight (not everyone reacts to bites).
  • Pale shed skins and tiny white eggs in seams, piping, headboard joints and cracks.
  • Live bugs in the seams or corners of furniture, often in clusters.
  • A sweet, musty odor in heavier infestations.

What you can do right now

  • Stay in the room you've been sleeping in so you don't carry bugs to a new one.
  • Bag bedding and clothes and run them through a hot dryer for at least 30 minutes.
  • Don't throw furniture on the curb without wrapping and marking it — that's how neighbors get them.
  • Skip foggers and store sprays; they scatter bed bugs into walls and adjoining rooms.
  • Inspect luggage and used furniture before it comes inside.

Frequently asked questions

Can bed bugs live in a clean house?

Yes. Bed bugs feed on blood, not crumbs or dirt, so a spotless home is just as vulnerable. Clutter only gives them more places to hide and makes treatment harder.

How long can bed bugs live without feeding?

Adults can survive for months without a blood meal, especially in cooler conditions. That is why leaving a room empty for a few weeks does not clear it.

Do bed bugs spread disease?

They are not known to transmit disease to people. The health effects are the bites themselves, skin infections from scratching, and the stress and sleep loss an infestation causes.
