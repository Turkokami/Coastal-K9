European Starling — Identification & Control

The European starling is the chunky, short-tailed black bird that roosts in huge, noisy flocks and nests in any cavity it can find — soffits, gable vents, dryer and bath vents, signs and gaps behind fascia boards. It was introduced to North America in 1890 and is now one of the continent's most common birds.

Quick answer: chattering and scratching inside a vent or soffit in spring is very often a starling nest. Fix it by removing the nest after the young leave and screening the opening — otherwise the same hole gets used every year.

How to identify the European starling

FeatureWhat to look for
Size & shapeAbout 8–9 inches, stocky, short square tail, pointed wings (triangular in flight)
Breeding plumageGlossy black with green-purple sheen and a yellow bill
Winter plumageCovered in white spots, darker bill
Look-alikesGrackles and blackbirds (longer tails; native and protected)

Behavior and biology

Starlings nest in cavities from early spring, often raising more than one brood. Outside nesting season they gather into very large communal roosts in trees, on buildings and under bridges and canopies, flying out each day to feed on insects, fruit, grain and livestock feed.

Why starlings are a problem

  • Nests in vents block dryer, bath and kitchen exhausts — a fire and moisture risk — and bring in mites.
  • Roosts concentrate heavy droppings on buildings, sidewalks and vehicles.
  • Agriculture. Flocks feed on and contaminate livestock feed and fruit crops.
  • Native birds. Starlings take over nest cavities from native species such as bluebirds and woodpeckers.

Signs of starlings

  • Birds flying in and out of a vent, soffit gap or sign in spring.
  • Nesting material — grass, twigs, feathers — sticking out of openings.
  • Loud chattering from chicks inside a wall or vent.
  • Droppings building up under a roost area in fall and winter.

How we handle starlings

Once a nest is inactive we clear the nesting material, clean the opening and close it with durable guards and screening sized for vents, then protect roost areas with netting, wire or spikes. Starlings are a non-native species not covered by the Migratory Bird Treaty Act, but correct identification matters — many similar-looking blackbirds are protected. Frequently asked questions

Why do starlings keep nesting in my dryer vent?

An open or louver-style vent is a perfect cavity. Once a pair uses it, birds return to the same spot. A proper vent guard that still lets air out is the long-term fix.

Are starlings protected?

European starlings are non-native and are not protected under the federal Migratory Bird Treaty Act. Native blackbirds and grackles that look similar are protected.
