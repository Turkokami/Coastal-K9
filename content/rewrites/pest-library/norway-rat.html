Norway Rat — Identification & Control

The Norway rat is the large brown rat that burrows at ground level around foundations, crawlspaces, dumpsters and loading docks. It is the most common commensal rat in the Southeast and the species behind most of the large burrow systems we excavate on commercial sites.

Quick answer: fresh holes about 2–4 inches across in bare soil along a foundation, wall or slab — with smooth, packed edges and a worn path between them — are almost always Norway rat burrows. Baiting or gassing only the openings you can see rarely works, because each system has hidden escape holes.

Behavior and biology

Norway rats dig. A typical burrow runs a few feet long and usually less than 18 inches deep, with a main entrance and one or more lightly plugged bolt holes for escape. Colonies build these systems under slabs, along retaining walls, beneath dumpster pads and in landscaping beds — anywhere loose soil sits next to steady food and water.

They are wary of new objects (neophobic), follow the same runways night after night, and usually forage within about 100–150 feet of the nest. A female can have several litters a year of six to twelve young, and the young are breeding themselves within about three months, so a small problem becomes a large one in a single season. A rat can squeeze through a gap about the size of a quarter, and they are strong swimmers that come up through sewer lines and floor drains.

Why Norway rats are a problem

They contaminate food and packaging, gnaw wiring and plumbing, undermine slabs, sidewalks and retaining walls with their tunnels, and carry pathogens such as leptospirosis and salmonella. On food plants and distribution warehouses, rodent activity is one of the fastest ways to fail a third-party audit. In apartment complexes and restaurants, the burrow system under the dumpster pad is often the source of the rats seen inside.

Signs of an infestation

  • Burrow openings in bare soil along foundations, walls, slabs and fence lines, often with fresh dirt fanned out.
  • Runways — narrow worn paths through grass or mulch between burrows and food.
  • Capsule-shaped droppings along walls and near food.
  • Greasy rub marks where they repeatedly brush against walls and pipes.
  • Gnawing on doors, pallets, bins and plastic, and nighttime scratching in walls or crawlspaces.

Prevention you can do

  • Keep dumpster lids closed and pads clean; fix leaking compactors and drains.
  • Store feed, birdseed and pet food in sealed metal or heavy plastic containers.
  • Clear dense vegetation, debris and stacked material from the first few feet around buildings.
  • Seal gaps around pipes and utilities with metal mesh, sheet metal or concrete — rats chew through foam.
  • Fit door sweeps and keep loading doors closed when not in use.

Frequently asked questions

Why don't rats take the bait I put out?

Norway rats avoid new objects and new foods at first, and they will keep feeding on a reliable food source like an open dumpster instead. Bait works best after sanitation removes the competition — and only once you know where the active burrows are.

Should I fill rat holes with dirt?

Filling holes and checking which ones reopen overnight is a useful way to tell active burrows from old ones. On its own it won't solve the problem — the colony simply digs a new entrance.

Are rat burrows dangerous to my slab or wall?

They can be. Large systems remove soil from under sidewalks, slabs and retaining walls, which leads to cracking and settling. That is why we restore the ground after a burrow system is cleared.
