Roof Rat — Identification & Control

The roof rat is the slender, agile climber of the rat world. In the CSRA it lives up high — in attics, soffits, wall voids, trees, ivy and palm-style landscaping — and travels along fences, utility lines and overhanging limbs to get onto the roof.

Quick answer: scratching overhead at night, pointed droppings in the attic, and gnawed holes at the roofline usually mean roof rats. They are solved at the roof and the tree line, not in the yard.

How to identify the roof rat

FeatureWhat to look for
BodySlender, about 6–8 inches plus tail
TailLonger than the head and body together
HeadPointed nose, large thin ears, prominent eyes
ColorDark gray to black, or brown with a white-to-gray belly
DroppingsAbout 1/2 inch, spindle-shaped with pointed ends
Look-alikesNorway rat (heavier, blunt nose, short tail, lives at ground level); flying and gray squirrels in attics

Behavior and biology

Roof rats are excellent climbers that prefer to nest above ground. Outdoors they use dense vines, tree canopies and woodpiles; indoors they head for attics, insulation, ceiling voids and the tops of cabinets. They are more sensitive to cold than Norway rats, which is why they are most common across the warmer Southeast and coastal South.

Like Norway rats they are cautious around new objects and breed quickly — several litters a year of about five to eight young. They feed heavily on fruit, nuts, seeds, pet food and birdseed, and they gnaw constantly to keep their incisors worn.

Why roof rats are a problem

Gnawed wiring in attics is a fire risk, and nesting rats contaminate insulation with droppings and urine. They chew through soffits, vents and roof returns, and they can carry the same diseases and parasites as other commensal rodents. Aiken's mature tree canopy — Hitchcock Woods edges, the Winter Colony, Houndslake — gives them highways straight onto rooflines.

Signs of an infestation

  • Scratching or running sounds in the ceiling or walls at dusk and overnight.
  • Pointed droppings in the attic, on top of cabinets or along rafters.
  • Gnaw holes at soffits, roof returns, gable vents and where utility lines enter.
  • Rub marks on beams and along the path from a tree limb to the roof.
  • Half-eaten fruit or nuts under trees and hollowed-out citrus or pecans.

Prevention you can do

  • Trim tree limbs back at least several feet from the roof and keep vines off walls and fences.
  • Pick up fallen fruit and nuts and stop feeding birds where seed spills.
  • Screen gable, soffit and ridge vents with sturdy metal mesh.
  • Seal gaps where cables, pipes and AC lines enter the house.

Frequently asked questions

Is it a rat or a squirrel in my attic?

Squirrels are mostly active during the day, especially early morning; roof rats are active at night. Droppings help too: roof rat droppings are small and pointed, squirrel droppings are larger and rounder.

Will poison bait in the attic fix it?

It often creates a new problem — rats that die in wall voids cause odor and flies — and it doesn't stop new rats coming in. Sealing the entry points is the part that makes the result last.
